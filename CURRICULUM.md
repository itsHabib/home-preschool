# The pedagogy: why the binder looks like this

*For a child around three who loves dinosaurs. Built to grow with them, not to
recreate a classroom.*

This is the *why* behind the binder and the weekly print-and-play kits; the binder
holds the what-to-do. **The theme is a swappable costume over a fixed skill
ladder.** The dinosaurs are the costume. The five strands below are the curriculum,
and if dinosaurs ever give way to the next obsession, keep the ladders and swap the
costume.

## The one idea everything hangs on

At this age you are **not** teaching letters and numbers. You are building the
*soil* they grow in: a big spoken vocabulary, a love of books, the discovery that
print and quantity *mean something*, and the feeling that learning with you is the
best part of the day. Letters and numbers are seeds dropped into that soil all day
long, in 2–10 minute bursts, through play and talk. Never a lesson a child has to
sit for.

Two mindset shifts make this better than a worksheet packet:

- **Reciting is not understanding.** Many three-year-olds can sing "1-2-3…10". That
  is rote counting. The real skill is that counting tells you *how many*: touching
  one object per number word (one-to-one correspondence), and knowing the last
  number you said is the total (cardinality). We build *that*, not a bigger recital.
- **Sounds before letter names.** A letter means nothing in A-B-C order. It means
  something when it is the sound at the start of *sun*. Every letter is introduced
  by its sound ("sss"), never its name ("ess"), starting with the S-A-T-P-I-N set,
  because those six sounds let a child blend real words within a few weeks. The
  child's name is printed on every page as a friendly thread; the letter of the
  week comes from what unlocks reading.

## What we deliberately drop from the classroom version

- **Calendar, day-of-the-week and weather-chart time.** There is no temporal
  framework yet at three; it is filler that looks like school.
- **A fifteen-minute sit-down circle time.** Attention at this age runs 2–5 minutes
  at a stretch. Learning folds into play and daily life instead.
- **Letter-of-the-week in alphabet order.** Replaced by sound-first, in the order
  that lets words get blended soonest.
- **Rote counting as the goal.** Replaced by counting real things with meaning.
- **Pages that hand over the answer.** Every page has one decision only the child
  can make.
- **Pretend as the lesson.** "Act like a dinosaur" is a garnish, never the day's
  activity. The main event is always something the child *makes or manipulates*:
  playdough, magnet tiles, cut cards, sorting.
- **Fancy color shades.** No fern versus olive. One plain color word per week.

## The daily rhythm (not a schedule)

Small children thrive on a predictable *flow*, not a clock. Anchor the day on
things that already happen, and fold tiny learning moments into the gaps.

1. **Breakfast.** Talk about what is coming: "first we get dressed, *then* outside."
2. **Book moment one.** Two or three books after breakfast. This is the single
   highest-value ten minutes of the day.
3. **Play, plus one tiny learning moment.** One 2–5 minute thing: hunt the week's
   sound, count the stairs as you climb, spot the week's color.
4. **Outside, big dinosaur stomps.** Thirty to sixty minutes. Movement *is*
   learning at this age.
5. **Lunch.** Count and compare the food: "you have *more* grapes than me."
6. **Quiet time or nap.**
7. **The day's page and card.** The one set-out activity. Pull the card onto the
   fridge in the morning and set out its page.
8. **Book moment two.** Bedtime books.

If a busy day is one book and one roar, that is a successful day. Keep that.

## The five strands

Every week carries all five. Each strand has a ladder in
[docs/curriculum-tree.md](docs/curriculum-tree.md) with a two-minute observable
check for every rung, so you know what to look for and when to nudge up.

### R. Reading

Sound-first and multisensory; never a pencil-and-paper drill. The ladder runs:
enjoys books and notices print → hears the first sound in a familiar spoken word
("*sss*-un") → knows the first six sounds → blends spoken sounds into a word
("c…a…t") → builds and reads short words with letter tiles → reads a decodable
sentence and says what happened.

### N. Number

Meaning, not recital. Count real things all day: eggs, horns, footprints, family
members. Keep "how many?" questions to sets of three or four at first, which is
where a three-year-old can actually succeed. The ladder runs: one touch per number
word → stops at the amount requested ("give me four") → sees two or three without
counting → matches quantities to numerals → compares groups (more, fewer, same) →
adds within five with objects. Written numerals ride along casually and are never
pushed.

### L. Language and stories

Oral vocabulary plus being read to is the strongest long-term predictor of reading,
more than letters and more than numbers. Narrate your day out loud. Use *real*
words ("that's an *ankylosaurus*", not "the big one"); dinosaur names are giant,
delicious vocabulary. Read the same favorites over and over; repetition is the
feature. Ask real questions: "what do you think happens next?" The ladder runs:
names things → uses short sentences → fills in the next line of a familiar book →
retells a story in order → explains with "because".

### M. Motor

The on-ramp for everything on paper. The ladder runs: closes a rough circle with a
fat crayon → draws a controlled line between two targets → traces a letter → cuts
on a line with safety scissors → writes their own name. Until the controlled line
arrives, every kit page is pair-the-cards or place-the-piece, never draw-the-line.

### T. Thinking

The strand that makes a child work. The ladder runs: continues an ABAB pattern →
names what is missing from a covered row → finds the odd one out and says why →
sorts by two attributes → continues an ABC pattern.

## Invitations, not tasks

Every parent card offers two ways in ("want to color Stegosaurus, or roll a dough
loop beside it?"). The child may take either, or neither. Play nearby for 2–10
minutes, hands first, on the floor. Stop on pushback and save it for another day.
Never move the child's hand, supply the answer, or require finishing. The card also
carries one harder rung for a child who is still hungry, and one optional question
about what happened, for the parent who feels like writing a line down.

## "Are worksheets good?" The honest answer

At three, the fine-motor control for real pencil work is still forming. Sustained
seatwork is a developmental mismatch, and it mostly teaches a child that learning
is boring.

Paper that *plays* is a different thing, and it is what the kits are made of: one
page a day, each tied to that day's activity, and each with one decision only the
child can make.

- **Count-and-match** cards: find the card with the same number of eggs.
- **Shadow match**: which silhouette belongs to this dinosaur?
- **Pattern strips**: leaf, egg, leaf, egg, and then?
- **Odd one out**: four dinosaurs, one eats meat.
- **Feed the dino**: the right food, and the right amount, in playdough.
- **Coloring pages** with the dinosaur's name and the week's sound.
- **Mini-books** the adult reads, with a sound choice and a page to make a mark on.

Fingers, fat crayons and playdough; no pencils, and nothing to get "right". Pop the
pages in sheet protectors and they are reusable.

## The book basket

Each week's binder page lists its own basket of three books and three songs, all
real and all checked (see [docs/sources.md](docs/sources.md)). Beyond the dinosaur
shelf, keep these classics in rotation:

- **Brown Bear, Brown Bear, What Do You See?** — Martin and Carle *(color,
  prediction, rhythm)*
- **Chicka Chicka Boom Boom** — Martin *(letters, playful sounds)*
- **Dear Zoo** — Rod Campbell *(prediction, lift-the-flap)*
- **The Very Hungry Caterpillar** — Eric Carle *(counting, sequence)*
- **We're Going on a Bear Hunt** — Rosen and Oxenbury *(movement, sound words)*
- **Moo, Baa, La La La!** — Sandra Boynton *(sounds, silliness)*
